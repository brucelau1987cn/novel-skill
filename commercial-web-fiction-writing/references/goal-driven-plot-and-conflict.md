# Goal-Driven Plot and Competitive Conflict

Use this reference when building plot-flow stories, stage arcs, contest scenes, upgrades, quests, reversals, or payoff chains.

## Goal Architecture

Start with an inciting event that establishes the protagonist's long-term goal. The inciting event may be dramatized or briefly narrated depending on genre, pace, and whether the event itself is the best entry point.

Build goals at three levels:

- Long-term goal: supplies sustained pursuit and a high-value final payoff.
- Stage goal: marks the current arc and converts the long-term goal into a concrete milestone.
- Short-term task: supplies frequent progress, setbacks, and immediate satisfaction.

Every smaller goal must have a clear necessity: why it moves the protagonist toward the larger goal and what failure costs. The promised reward must be paid in full. A rescue goal should genuinely rescue the person; a treasure goal should deliver the core treasure. Do not replace a promised payoff with a materially weaker substitute.

## Reusable Plot Unit

Use this sequence as a flexible unit:

`goal -> action -> obstacle -> reversal -> success/consequence`

- Action: the protagonist makes a choice and pursues a necessary step. Plant usable people, objects, information, or relationship details here.
- Obstacle: disrupt the expected route, revise the protagonist's understanding, remove an escape route, or raise the cost of failure.
- Reversal: call back a planted element; do not rely on an unprepared coincidence.
- Success/consequence: pay off the pressure with a proportionate reward, loss, new status, or next problem.

This structure can recurse. The action phase of a large arc may contain several complete smaller units. Do not force it onto weak-goal, atmosphere-first, or novelty-first stories whose actual reader engine is different.

## Goal Variants

### Urgent Goal

Add a deadline, pursuit, resource depletion, or irreversible consequence:

`goal + countdown + continuing interference + severe failure cost`

### Partial Completion and Turn

Pay part of the old promise, then expose a deeper injury, trap, price, or requirement. This creates stage satisfaction while naturally establishing the next goal. Do not use a turn to revoke the reward readers were explicitly promised.

### Accidental Secondary Progress

Completing goal A may logically advance goal B, producing a bonus payoff. Use sparingly and make the link causal and consistent with established rules.

## Competitive Conflict

The base structure is simple: a scarce target appears, multiple parties want it, their actions collide, and one party obtains it. The useful design variable is **competitive intent**.

Score each participant on:

1. Reward value and scarcity.
2. Competitive or restrained temperament.
3. Urgency of personal need.
4. Relationship to rivals: affection may reduce competition; hatred may produce spiteful competition.

Do not assume everyone wants the prize equally.

## Contrast-Based Variants

- Reward contrast: the prize looks worthless but has hidden value; ridicule becomes a later bargain-and-face-slap payoff.
- Character contrast: a supposedly detached person secretly wants everything but cannot act without breaking their public persona.
- Purpose contrast: the visible contest is bait; the real purpose is to lure or expose an enemy.

## Raising the Limit

Use extreme behavior to reveal value and character:

- A normally noncompetitive person entering proves the reward's value without exposition.
- An honorable rival who refuses dirty tactics despite desperate need becomes a respected opponent and raises the value of the protagonist's victory.
- A compulsive rival willing to self-destruct for the target supplies maximum volatility.

Maximum raw intensity comes from: extremely scarce target, extremely high intent on both sides, urgent need, competitive personalities, and hostile relations. Not every scene should maximize all five; lowering one variable can create restraint, surprise, sacrifice, or strategic asymmetry.

## Planning Table

For important competitions, record:

| Character | Visible goal | Real goal | Need level | Maximum price | Moral/tactical boundary |
|---|---|---:|---:|---|---|

A strong contest changes more than ownership. It should expose a true face, test a principle, alter a relationship, release a promised emotion, or launch the next plot node.
