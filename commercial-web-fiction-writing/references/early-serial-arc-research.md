# Early Serial Arc Research: Chapters 1–20

Use this reference when studying commercial web-fiction beyond the opening three chapters or when planning/reviewing a new book's first 15–20 chapters.

## Evidence Boundary

Record the exact readable range per work. Do not claim 20 chapters when access stops earlier. A visible title, HTTP 200 response, catalogue entry, VIP marker, or recommendation preview is not evidence that the target chapter body was read.

When a platform exposes different mobile, API, and desktop reading paths, treat their catalogue and trial boundaries separately. Follow canonical next-chapter links rather than assuming chapter IDs are sequential. Verify the target article container actually contains target-work prose before counting a chapter.

For each chapter, retain only compact research metadata:

- chapter number and title;
- body length and paragraph/dialogue indicators when useful;
- protagonist and key-mechanism presence;
- opening event and final action/sentence, kept short;
- state change, payoff type, and hook type.

Do not store full competitor chapters. Do not persist cookies, tokens, phone numbers, OTPs, or account identifiers.

## Analysis Windows

Analyze each work in three windows rather than chapter-by-chapter plot retelling:

### Chapters 1–3: Establish the New State

Check whether the old state becomes untenable, the protagonist proves change through action, and the local disruption connects to a long-term task.

### Chapters 4–10: Pay the First Promise

Check whether the title/blurb's first concrete action is performed, whether old relationships or rules react, and whether the story starts a second engine before the first conflict exhausts itself.

### Chapters 11–20: Cross the Engines

Check whether the new engine causally affects the original main line, whether early resources and information are reused, whether a small hook is paid off, and whether the arc forms a stage closure plus a strong next-stage entry.

Chapter numbers are observation bands, not platform rules. Genre contract and scene integrity take priority.

## Five-Variable Progress Test

A repeated conflict should change at least one variable:

1. Information: who knows what, and who still misreads it.
2. Relationship: trust, distance, control, duty, or alignment.
3. Resources: money, tools, ability, status, allies, or permission.
4. Map: household, workplace, journey, institution, or instance.
5. Cost: rising danger, shorter deadline, lost retreat, or added duty.

Three similar confrontations without a variable change usually need deletion, compression, or reconstruction.

## Engine Relay

A second engine is not an unrelated subplot. It must exchange causality with the first engine.

Examples of valid relay shapes:

- relationship withdrawal creates a career return and forces the old family to notice the missing labor;
- a new marriage/status supplies legal or social resources for a family-rescue goal;
- completing one instance grants the assets, knowledge, or eligibility required for the next while advancing a cross-instance mystery;
- survival logistics grow into organization, production, and political positioning.

## Promise-Debt Ledger

Track every high-value title/blurb promise through:

`first forecast -> first proof -> stage payoff -> full payoff`

If full payoff must be delayed, require continuing same-direction evidence. Specific promise words carry heavier debt. A story titled around marriage, professional ability, management status, or large-scale production must let that mechanism affect events, not remain a label.

## Payoff Rotation

Tag payoffs when useful:

- `D`: decision/control regained;
- `R`: relationship recognition or change;
- `A`: ability demonstrated;
- `S`: resource/status gained;
- `K`: knowledge or rule revealed.

Do not substitute one payoff for another. A verbal win does not complete a separation; defeating one monster does not complete an instance; a powerful partner's rescue does not replace the protagonist's decisive choice.

## Protagonist Four-Rights Test

For each decisive unit, identify who holds:

- discovery right: finds the problem, clue, or opportunity;
- choice right: decides the direction;
- action right: executes the solution;
- consequence right: bears the cost and receives the change.

Supporting characters may help, but should not repeatedly monopolize decisive functions. High appearance frequency and abundant internal monologue do not compensate for missing agency.

## Ability, Resource, and Authority Ledgers

For abilities, record conditions, consumption, recovery, obligation, exposure risk, and ethical/legal consequences. A repeated fainting scene is not a durable cost unless it changes later choices.

For authority-bearing helpers such as systems, administrators, patrons, senior allies, or backstage groups, also record the scope, approval path, competing interests, and failure modes of their intervention. If they can revise rules, grant credentials, dispatch aid, or erase consequences whenever convenient, the story has converted tension into protected access.

For management and survival stories, separate:

- public ledger: explainable resources, professional competence, and social credit;
- hidden ledger: space inventory, private intelligence, forbidden tools, or secret authority.

Hidden resources require concealment cost. Rewards should be reused in later choices; one-use prizes weaken the sense of accumulation.

## Hook Rotation

Classify chapter endings as danger, new information, relationship judgment, action launch, character intrusion, or emotional landing. Hard cliffhangers are optional. The durable standard is:

- the chapter changes the state;
- the next chapter has a concrete unresolved problem.

Repeated use of the same ending mechanism produces template fatigue.

## Comparative Synthesis

For each work, report:

1. promise and primary engine;
2. 1–3, 4–10, and 11–20 progression;
3. protagonist agency;
4. hook and payoff rotation;
5. ability/resource accounting;
6. three transferable methods;
7. two or more logic, repetition, ethics, or imitation risks.

Separate observed evidence from interpretation. Learn mechanisms and genre contracts, not distinctive props, complete causal chains, signature scenes, or unique character combinations.
